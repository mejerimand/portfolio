Following my bachelor's, I interned at Peech — a place that at first glance is a regular sex shop. However, Peech sells sex toys the way other shops sell kitchenware: openly, without apology, and with a politics attached. I joined as a concept and product developer, and the work I ended up doing turned out to be less about the product than about the field it had to live in.

To work with the sensitive subjects and user-groups of Peech, I employed participatory design to develop concepts and map Peech's socio-technical arena. This surfaced user groups the brand's own statistics couldn't see: The shameful consumer, who has a sexual life and wants it delivered in an unmarked box. The unheard consumer, too old for the brand's Instagram to reach. The queer consumer, for whom the language on the packaging is the product. In the middle of this mapping, I found a controversy at the centre: a shop that trades on activist credibility has to keep asking whether it is helping the cause or monetising it.

In my time at Peech, I developed three concepts, one of which was taken to production (although after my time at Peech had ended): a foreplay card game built in three levels of intensity, with non-monogamous and gender-neutral language throughout. This project was interesting, because the bar for both producing it (from Peech's point of view) and buying it (from the consumer's point of view) was low enough to be challenging to binary and gendered norms, meaning it might end up with users not intentionally buying it for its norm-challenging features. At the same time, using this platform to be challenging in these aspects was new, as no competitor had developed a similar kind of game.

However, I found that this was not the most important work I could have done in this arena. I would argue that another concept I had presented, but was never developed further, was a game about self-acceptance and the norms we judge ourselves by (following my research from my bachelor's thesis). Even if this was a better intervention, this concept simply was not perceived as 'sexy' enough to sell, which highlights an issue that finally made me want to switch into design research: the gap between design that would be able to drive the most capital and the design that would have the most impact.
